What we build

One store, one rule about who may read a fact.

The brief says what the month produces and how each claim gets checked. This page says what the thing actually is. It is the design Tideline v2 is built to, written so it can be argued with — every mechanism, what it is for, and where it came from.

The shape

Personal memory and company memory are the same mechanism.

The obvious way to build a company brain is two stores: a private one per person and a shared one for the organization, with something that copies between them. Every product in the field is built that way, and it is the source of their leaks — the copy step is where a private fact quietly becomes a public one, and where a summary of five private facts becomes readable by someone who could not read any of the five.

Tideline has one store. Every fact carries a label, which is nothing more than the set of people who may read it. alice is a label. team:payments is a label. company is a label. "Personal memory" is the view of the store where the label is one person; "company memory" is the view where the label is everyone. There is no copy step, because there is nothing to copy between.

Labels hold references to groups, not lists of people. When someone leaves the payments team, the directory changes and every payments fact becomes invisible to them on their next question — no re-indexing, no propagation delay. The incumbents rebuild an index instead: between thirty minutes and, on a large enough site, more than a week.

fact value "deploy target is Thandrel Infra" valid from 14 Aug, until — label team:platform provenance ← msg#8821, fact#4410 depends on fact#4410, rule: "logs drain follows the deploy target" written by dilip, via slack connector

Six fields. Everything the protocol guarantees follows from these and from one rule: a fact made out of other facts is readable only by people who could read all of them.

The eight rules

Each one is a property test, not a paragraph.

These are written as executable checks in Phase 1 and stay green for the life of the system. A store that passes them cannot leak a private fact into company memory, cannot report a stale value as current, and cannot be probed through the provider's cache — whatever is built on top of it later.

I1

A fact from an untrusted source cannot overwrite a trusted one.

Tool output, a retrieved web page and a distilled summary are all untrusted by default. None of them may author an identity, a preference or a correction, and none may supersede something a person said.

Why: this is how a poisoned document rewrites what the company believes. Tideline has the gate today; two paths bypass it and get closed.

I2

A derived fact is as restricted as its most restricted source.

Summaries, consolidations, dream-pass merges and generated pages take the intersection of their sources' readers. Consolidation runs inside a label, never across.

Why: otherwise summarising is a laundering machine — the documented failure in Glean's project artifacts, Copilot's generated documents and Salesforce's audit objects. Prior art: Denning 1976; Microsoft's Fides states exactly this rule for model outputs.

I3

What you may not read does not exist for you.

The candidate set is filtered by label before ranking, never after. Two stores that agree on what you may read return identical answers — same content, same count, same order, same scores, same time.

Why: filtering after retrieval leaks through what is missing, how many results came back and how long it took. Measured at 86% structural leakage in the published study.

I4

The company block is cached once for everyone; private blocks cannot be probed.

Prompt blocks are ordered from most public to most private, and a boundary marker known only to the compartment's members sits at each step up. The organization-wide memory is byte-identical for every employee, so the provider caches it once and everyone reads it cheaply.

Why: providers cache by exact prefix across the whole account. Without this, either private facts sit in a cache other employees can time — a published attack — or nothing is shared and everyone pays full price. Prior art: the same construction exists one layer down, in vLLM's cache-salting proposal; this is it applied to a black-box provider by an assembler that knows the labels.

I5

When a fact changes, everything that depended on it recomputes or says it no longer knows.

Dependency edges carry the rule in the words it was written in. On supersession, the store walks only the affected dependents: with a rule, it computes the new value; without one, it marks the dependent unknown and asks for confirmation. No model pass over the store.

Why: this is the failure the whole field shares — 3% and 1% on the two public tasks that test it. Prior art: truth maintenance from 1979, incremental view maintenance from 1993, and this year MemoRepair and MemTX. What is open is doing it at ordinary cost, which is the number Phase 2 exists to produce.

I6

Deleting a fact deletes what was built from it, and leaves a receipt.

Purge follows provenance to every descendant, destroys the per-fact key, and appends a receipt to a hash-chained log that a third party can verify without seeing any content.

Why: departures and erasure requests. Tideline has the field for provenance and nothing writes it, so the cascade is currently inert — that is a Phase 2 fix, and its quality is bounded by the edge-recall number from Phase 0.

I7

"Could this person have known this, on that day?" is answerable from the log.

Three clocks on every fact: when it was true in the world, when the store learned it, and when the reader was entitled to see it. Membership changes and declassifications are events in the same log.

Why: it is the question asked in an incident review and by a regulator, and no memory product can answer it — Copilot's own documentation says memory actions generate no audit entries at all.

I8

A fact's audience grows only through a door, and every contributor holds a key.

Nothing widens automatically. Publishing something to a wider audience requires the authority of everyone whose facts it was built from — not merely whoever touched it last — and produces a receipt.

Why: the alternative is that a manager's summary of five private notes silently becomes company knowledge. Prior art: robust declassification (2001); MemTX blocks the same move at commit time. The all-contributors rule is ours.

Company memory

Five doors in, and nothing else.

"Company memory" is the label everyone can read. A fact can arrive there in exactly five ways, each one audited. Anything else is a bug and the conformance suite fails.

It was already public.

A public channel, the handbook, an org-wide document. The label comes from the channel it arrived through — never from reading the content and guessing.

The owner published it.

A person moves their own fact outward, signed, with a receipt. An agent cannot do this on their behalf without an explicit approval step.

An administrator promoted it.

Logged as an override, with the owner notified. Available, visible, and never silent.

It was derived from things already public.

Automatic, and safe precisely because of I2 — the derived label is the intersection, so this door can only ever produce something at most as wide as its narrowest source.

It is a statistic, not a fact.

Aggregate release under a privacy budget, for questions like "how often does this come up" where no individual record should be readable. Opt-in, off by default.

The three models

Where a guarantee replaces a guess.

Belief with four values

A fact is true, false, contradicted, or no-longer-known. Merging follows the algebra rather than a status flag, which is what gives "I don't know any more" a definition instead of a hopeful sentence in a prompt. Feeds I5.

Trust from agreement

Source reliability and fact truth estimated together, converging provably. Replaces the current rule that promotes a belief after three reads — a loop where being used makes something true. Sources are people, tools and channels, which already carry labels.

Injection under a budget

Instead of "send the top five", send the set that holds retrieval quality above a bound for the dollars available, with a distribution-free guarantee. Turns "we cut tokens" into "we stayed within this much of the best possible for this budget".

None of the three is a new theorem, and the brief does not claim one. The information-theoretic bound on how small memory can get was published in July by other people. What is open in all three cases is the operating point — nobody has run them over a labelled, time-aware, dependency-carrying store and reported what it costs.

How it ships

Tideline is the part that is sold.

Tideline is the store and this protocol: labels, doors, propagation, receipts, cache placement, and the numbers. It is private, and it is what an enterprise buys.

Grokbot is the workspace people see — agents, channels, the admin console. In protocol terms it is a client: it proves who you are, runs agents on your behalf rather than as principals of their own, and holds no memory itself. Every write goes through the store, so the workspace cannot launder anything into company memory even by accident.

Living pages are the visible payoff. A page is a derived fact: it carries the intersection label of its sources, so you only see pages built from things you may read, and it updates itself through I5 when a fact under it changes. A self-updating company wiki and correctness-under-change are the same mechanism, seen from two ends.

Brigade stays public and MIT. The memory core it ships with remains open. Tideline v2 is a separate private package that plugs into the memory slot Brigade already exposes, so the enterprise build is upstream Brigade plus one directory. Plumbing goes upstream where everyone benefits; the protocol and the models stay private.
