Prior art

What already exists, and the little that does not.

Before writing a line of the design we searched for every piece of it — in the peer-reviewed literature and in this year's preprints. Most of it exists. Three ideas we had were published while we were working, and one was published in July. This page is the honest accounting, because a claim of novelty that a reviewer can break in an afternoon costs more than making no claim at all.

All four are cited in the design rather than claimed. The cache work still matters to us — it is prior art one layer down, in the inference server, where the salt is an API parameter. Nobody has done it above a black-box provider, driven by the labels on the memory itself.

The core mechanism

Cascading repair was published three times this year.

In May, a paper named the problem — the cascade update problem — and formalised repair as a minimum-cut over the graph of derived artifacts, reporting that with complete provenance it drives invalidated-memory exposure from between seventy and ninety-four percent down to zero, and that one percent of missing edges leaves seventeen percent still reachable.

In July, another built a transactional commit protocol for agent memory where each record carries evidence, permissions, provenance and validity; retraction triggers typed cascading repair; and the protocol was machine-checked over five and a half million states. It blocks, at commit time, exactly the move we call laundering: republishing a private parent into a wider scope.

In August, an open-source Datalog memory shipped with bi-temporal validity, provenance and recomputation of transitive dependents on retraction.

Behind all three: truth maintenance from 1979, assumption-based truth maintenance from 1986, and incremental view maintenance from 1993. The idea that a store should repair what depended on a retracted fact is older than most of the people building these products.

So what is left?

Three things, all of them empirical rather than theoretical:

  • The operating point. Keep the rule in the words it was written in, apply it with one narrow call rather than a rewrite of the store, and report what that costs. The only configuration that passes the public exam today does it with a frontier model rewriting the whole memory file, at roughly seventy times the baseline.
  • A number on the public exam. None of the three papers ran it. The vendor that publishes scores ran it on itself and says so.
  • The composition. Labels, time, dependencies, provenance and cost in one schema, with a conformance suite that holds all of it at once — the transactional paper has no cost column, the repair paper has no permissions, the open-source one has no evaluation.

The stance

A protocol, not an algorithm.

Nothing above supports a claim to have invented something. What it supports is a different and more durable claim: that these pieces have never been put in one schema, held together by invariants that execute, and measured on public exams with a cost column.

That is the shape of the strongest paper in this space — a protocol with machine-checked properties — and it is the shape an enterprise buyer can actually evaluate. "We invented a new algorithm" invites a reviewer to go looking. "Here are eight properties, here is the suite that proves them, here are the numbers on the public tests" does not.

The bibliography behind this page runs to sixty-five peer-reviewed entries, sixty of them with a resolved DOI, grouped by which property they support. Worth noting for calibration: no peer-reviewed survey of agent-memory security exists, and no peer-reviewed deterministic-cascade agent-memory paper exists. Several of the systems we cite as closest prior art are preprints only.
